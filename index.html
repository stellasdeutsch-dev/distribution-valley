What you need to know already

Only that a probability is a number from 0 to 1.

Everything else (Σ, factorials, “n choose k”, area under a curve, e) is explained in the toolbox below. Read one part, then close your eyes and say the “remember it” line out loud. That is how it sticks.

The basics

Three Ideas That Power Every Chapter

Before the six distributions, three words you will see on almost every slide.

Random variable

A number that depends on chance. X = your kills in the next teamfight. Before the fight you don't know it. You only know how likely each value is.

Discrete vs continuous

Discrete values can be counted (0, 1, 2 kills): bars, and the bars add up to 1. Continuous values can be anything (your ping, 23.7 ms): a curve, and the area under it is 1.

Mean and variance

Mean: the long-run average, the center. Variance: how far values usually land from the center. Consistent player: small variance. 40 kills one game, 3 the next: big variance.

Math toolbox

Five Small Tools You Will Use All Day

Each tool has a rule, plain words, a tiny Dota example and a “try it” question.

Tool 1

A distribution adds up to 1

A random variable X is a number that depends on chance, like your kills in the next fight. Its distribution lists every possible value and how likely it is. Something must happen, so all the probabilities together are exactly 1.

p(0) + p(1) + p(2) + … = 1
Try it: p(0) = 0.5, p(1) = 0.3. If 2 kills is the only other option, what is p(2)?

1 − 0.5 − 0.3 = 0.2.

Tool 2

Σ means “add them all”

The big Greek letter Σ (sigma) is short for a long sum. Σx x · p(x) means: for every possible value x, compute x · p(x), then add the results.

Σx x · p(x) = 0·p(0) + 1·p(1) + 2·p(2) + …
Try it: compute Σ x·p(x) for p(0)=0.5, p(1)=0.3, p(2)=0.2.

0 + 0.3 + 0.4 = 0.7. That is the mean, explained later.

Tool 3

n choose k: how many orders?

Mirana shoots 4 arrows and hits 2. In how many orders can that happen? List them: HHMM, HMHM, HMMH, MHHM, MHMH, MMHH. Six. The formula gives the same count:

C(n, k) = n! / (k! (n − k)!)    C(4, 2) = 24 / (2 · 2) = 6

n! (“n factorial”) means n · (n − 1) · … · 1, so 4! = 24, and 0! = 1.

Try it: C(3, 1)? List the orders too.

3!/(1!·2!) = 6/2 = 3: HMM, MHM, MMH.

Tool 4

For curves, probability is area

When X can be any number (ping in ms, a hit rate between 0 and 1), we draw a curve, the density. The probability that X lands between a and b is the area under the curve from a to b. The whole area is 1. You don't need calculus today: think “area of a shape”.

P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = area under p(x) from a to b
Try it: a flat density on [0, 4] has which height?

Width 4 × height h = 1, so h = 1/4.

Tool 5

The number e and exp

e ≈ 2.718 is a special constant, like π. exp(a) is just another way to write ea. What you need to know: exp(0) = 1, and exp(−big number) is close to 0. That is why the Gaussian formula with exp(−(x − μ)²/2σ²) is highest at x = μ and falls off on both sides.

Remember it: probabilities add up to 1 (bars) or have area 1 (curves); Σ means add; C(n, k) counts orders.

How to compute them

Mean & Variance, Step by Step

One small Dota example. Check every number with a calculator once and the formulas stop being scary.

E[X] = Σx x · p(x)Mean: each value times its probability, then add
Var(X) = E[X²] − (E[X])²Variance: mean of the squares minus the square of the mean

Say X = your kills in one teamfight, and from many teamfights you know:

Variance: E[X²] − 0.7² = 1.1 − 0.49 = 0.61. The standard deviation is √0.61 ≈ 0.78, in the same units as X (kills).

Continuous version: replace the sum with an integral, E[X] = ∫ x p(x) dx. Same idea, area instead of bars.

Two rules worth remembering

E[aX + b] = a·E[X] + bDouble every kill and add 1: the mean doubles and goes up by 1.
Var(aX + b) = a²·Var(X)Adding b moves the center but not the spread. Multiplying by a scales the spread by a².
Why variance uses squares

Why Not Just Average the Distances?

Take a player who gets 0 kills in half the fights and 2 kills in the other half. The mean is 1. Distances from the mean: 0 − 1 = −1 and 2 − 1 = +1. Their average is 0, as if there were no spread at all. Left and right cancel.

Squaring makes every distance positive: (−1)² = 1, (+1)² = 1. The average squared distance is 1. That is the variance:

Var(X) = E[(X − μ)²] = average squared distance from the mean

The shortcut E[X²] − μ² gives the same number and is faster by hand. Here: E[X²] = 0·0.5 + 4·0.5 = 2, so 2 − 1² = 1 ✓.

The standard deviation σ = √Var brings it back to normal units (kills, not kills²).

Without squares: −1 + 1 = 0, no spread. With squares: 1 + 1, the spread shows.

Remember it: mean = center (weighted average); variance = average squared distance from the center, squared so left and right don't cancel.

The six distributions, slowly

One Story, One Formula, One Calculation Each

For each: when do you use it, what does every piece of the formula mean, a small calculation, and the line to remember.

1

Bernoulli(θ): one yes/no

discrete

Story. One Mirana arrow: hit (X = 1) or miss (X = 0). θ is the chance of a hit.

p(x) = θx (1 − θ)1−x, x ∈ {0, 1}

Piece by piece. Put in x = 1: θ¹(1 − θ)⁰ = θ. Put in x = 0: θ⁰(1 − θ)¹ = 1 − θ. The powers are just a trick to write both cases in one formula.

meanE[X] = 0·(1 − θ) + 1·θ = θTool 2: value × probability, added
E[X²]0²·(1 − θ) + 1²·θ = θ0² = 0 and 1² = 1
varianceθ − θ² = θ(1 − θ)shortcut E[X²] − μ²

Remember it: one yes/no; mean θ, variance θ(1 − θ), which is biggest at θ = 0.5 (most unpredictable).

2

Binomial(n, θ): how many yes out of n

discrete

Story. Mirana shoots n arrows, each hits with chance θ, independently. X = number of hits.

p(k) = C(n, k) · θk · (1 − θ)n−k

Piece by piece. θk: k hits. (1 − θ)n−k: the other n − k miss. C(n, k): the number of orders (Tool 3), because HHMM and MHMH both count as “2 hits”.

examplen = 4, θ = 0.5: p(2) = 6 · 0.5² · 0.5² = 6/16 = 0.375C(4, 2) = 6 orders, each with probability 1/16
meanE[X] = nθ = 4 · 0.5 = 2n Bernoullis, each with mean θ: means add up
variancenθ(1 − θ) = 4 · 0.5 · 0.5 = 1independent shots: variances add up too

Remember it: Binomial = n Bernoullis added up. That is why its mean is nθ and its variance nθ(1 − θ).

3

Geometric(q): waiting for the first success

discrete

Story. You win each game with chance q. X = number of losses before your first win.

p(k) = (1 − q)k · q, k = 0, 1, 2, …

Piece by piece. Read it as a story in time: lose, lose, …, lose (k times, each 1 − q), then win (q). Independent games, so multiply. No C(n, k) here: there is only one order, the win always comes last.

exampleq = 0.25: p(0) = 0.25, p(1) = 0.75·0.25 ≈ 0.19, p(2) ≈ 0.14each extra loss multiplies by 0.75, so the bars shrink
meanE[X] = (1 − q)/q = 0.75/0.25 = 3you win 1 game in 4 on average, so about 3 losses come first
variance(1 − q)/q² = 12long losing streaks are possible, so the spread is big
Exam trap. Some books count games including the win: y = k + 1, p(y) = q(1 − q)y−1, mean 1/q. The 2025 final used the first version, the 2026 final the second. Always check where the counting starts.

Remember it: lose k times, then win: (1 − q)kq. Mean (1 − q)/q losses.

4

Categorical(π): one of K labels

discrete

Story. Which role does the enemy pick first? Five labels, each with its own probability πk (pi). It is a die with K sides that need not be fair.

p(X = k) = πk, π1 + … + πK = 1

No mean, no variance. The outcomes are names, not numbers. “The average of carry and mid” means nothing. Instead you talk about the most likely label (the mode).

roleπ
carry0.40
mid0.25
offlane0.15
soft support0.10
hard support0.10
sum1.00

Remember it: Bernoulli with K options instead of 2. Probabilities add to 1; no mean because labels are not numbers.

5

Beta(a, b): a belief about a probability

continuous

Story. You don't know a new Mirana's hit rate θ. The Beta distribution describes your belief about θ: which values between 0 and 1 seem likely.

p(θ) = θa−1 (1 − θ)b−1 / B(a, b), 0 < θ < 1

Piece by piece. θa−1(1 − θ)b−1 is the shape; it looks just like “a − 1 hits and b − 1 misses”. B(a, b) is only a number that makes the area equal to 1 (Tool 4). You never compute it by hand on the exam.

a = b = 1p(θ) = 1, flat“no idea”: every hit rate equally believable
a = 8, b = 4mean = 8/12 ≈ 0.67like having seen more hits than misses: the curve leans right
meanE[θ] = a/(a + b)“hits ÷ total” in pseudo-counts; it is on the formula sheet
widthbigger a + b → narrower curvemore (pseudo) data means more certainty

Remember it: Beta lives on [0, 1]; think a = hits, b = misses; mean a/(a + b); more total → narrower.

6

Gaussian N(μ, σ²): the bell curve

continuous

Story. Last hits at 10 minutes over many games: most games are near your usual number, a few are much higher or lower.

p(x) = (1/√(2πσ²)) · exp(−(x − μ)² / (2σ²))

Piece by piece. (x − μ)²: squared distance from the center μ. Divided by 2σ²: a large spread σ² makes far values less “expensive”. exp(−…): equals 1 at the center and drops toward 0 far away (Tool 5). 1/√(2πσ²): only makes the area 1.

meanE[X] = μthe curve is symmetric around μ
varianceVar(X) = σ²σ is the typical distance from the center
68–95 ruleN(60, 10²): 68% in [50, 70], 95% in [40, 80]within 1σ and 2σ of the center

Remember it: center μ, spread σ²; highest at μ, falls off by the squared distance; 68% within one σ.

FAQ

Common Questions About Distributions

The things beginners get stuck on. Open one at a time.

What is the difference between p(x) for discrete and continuous variables?

Discrete: p(x) is a real probability, like p(2 kills) = 0.2. Continuous: p(x) is a density. You only get a probability from an area: P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = ∫ab p(x) dx.

Can a density be bigger than 1?

Yes. A uniform distribution on [0, 0.5] has height 2, because the total area must be 1: width 0.5 × height 2 = 1. Only areas must stay at most 1.

What is the probability that a continuous X equals one exact value?

Zero. A single point has no width, so no area. P(ping = exactly 23.700000 ms) = 0, but P(ping between 23 and 24 ms) can be large.

Which version of the geometric distribution should I use?

Read the support. Failures before success (k = 0, 1, 2, …): (1 − q)k q, mean (1 − q)/q. Tries until success (y = 1, 2, …): λ(1 − λ)y−1, mean 1/λ. Both appeared on finals.

Why does the Beta distribution matter so much?

It is a distribution over a probability θ between 0 and 1, so it can describe your belief about an arrow hit rate. With Bernoulli, Binomial or Geometric data, a Beta prior gives a Beta posterior. That is tomorrow's and the day after tomorrow's topic.

Why is there no mean for the Categorical distribution?

Its outcomes are labels (carry, mid, offlane…), not numbers. The average of “mid” and “carry” makes no sense. You can still talk about the most likely label and each probability.

Check yourself

Five Quick Questions

Paper first, then open. Use the explorer to verify.

1. Bernoulli(0.3): mean and variance?

Mean θ = 0.3. Variance θ(1 − θ) = 0.3 · 0.7 = 0.21.

2. 10 Mirana arrows, hit rate 0.4. Expected hits and variance?

Binomial(10, 0.4): mean nθ = 4, variance nθ(1 − θ) = 10 · 0.4 · 0.6 = 2.4.

3. You win each game with probability 0.2. Expected games lost before the first win?

Geometric, failures version: (1 − q)/q = 0.8/0.2 = 4 games.

4. Your belief about an arrow hit rate is Beta(2, 8). What is its mean?

a/(a + b) = 2/10 = 0.2. You expect a 20% hit rate.

5. X has mean 0.7 and variance 0.61. What are the mean and variance of 2X + 1?

Mean 2 · 0.7 + 1 = 2.4. Variance 2² · 0.61 = 2.44. The “+1” does not change the spread.

What is a random variable? Say it with a Dota example.

A number that depends on chance, like your kills in the next teamfight. Before the fight you only know how likely each value is.

Why is the Binomial mean nθ?

A Binomial is n Bernoulli shots added up. Each shot has mean θ, and means add up, so n · θ.

Why does the Binomial formula have C(n, k) but the Geometric does not?

“2 hits out of 4” can happen in 6 orders, so we count them. “k losses, then the first win” has only one order: the win is always last.

Why can a density be bigger than 1, but a probability can't?

For curves, probability is area. A tall, thin shape can have height 4 and still area 1. Only areas must stay between 0 and 1.

What do a and b “feel like” in Beta(a, b)?

Hits and misses you have (pseudo-)seen. Mean a/(a + b) is hits over total, and more total makes the curve narrower: more certainty.

Practice · 30–40 min

Practice Problems for Today

Seven Dota tasks, from warm-up to exam-style. Solve on paper first, then open the hint or the solution.

1Easy

Kills in a teamfight

Your kills X in one teamfight: p(0) = 0.4, p(1) = 0.35, p(2) = 0.2, p(3) = 0.05. Find E[X], Var(X) and the standard deviation.

Hint

Make the table from the “Mean & variance” section: columns x·p(x) and x²·p(x).

Solution
  • E[X] = 0 + 0.35 + 0.4 + 0.15 = 0.9
  • E[X²] = 0 + 0.35 + 0.8 + 0.45 = 1.6
  • Var(X) = 1.6 − 0.81 = 0.79, sd ≈ 0.89
2Easy

Gold from a fight

In a made-up patch, each kill gives 200 gold and every fight gives 100 gold extra: G = 200X + 100, with X from Problem 1. Find E[G] and Var(G).

Hint

Use the two rules: E[aX + b] = aE[X] + b and Var(aX + b) = a²Var(X).

Solution
  • E[G] = 200·0.9 + 100 = 280 gold
  • Var(G) = 200²·0.79 = 31,600, sd ≈ 178 gold. The +100 does not change the spread.
3Medium

Twelve Mirana arrows

Mirana shoots 12 arrows, each hits with probability 0.25, independently. (a) Which distribution? Mean and variance? (b) P(no hits)? (c) P(exactly 1 hit)?

Hint

Binomial: p(k) = C(n, k) θk(1 − θ)n−k. C(12, 0) = 1, C(12, 1) = 12.

Solution
  • (a) Binomial(12, 0.25): mean 3, variance 12·0.25·0.75 = 2.25
  • (b) 0.7512 ≈ 0.032
  • (c) 12 · 0.25 · 0.7511 ≈ 0.127
4Medium

Two versions of the geometric

You win each game with probability q = 0.25. (a) Expected number of losses before the first win? (b) Expected number of games until the first win, including it? (c) Probability that the first win comes exactly in game 3?

Hint

Failures version: mean (1 − q)/q. Tries version: mean 1/q. For (c): lose, lose, win.

Solution
  • (a) 0.75/0.25 = 3 losses
  • (b) 1/0.25 = 4 games, which is (a) + 1
  • (c) 0.75 · 0.75 · 0.25 ≈ 0.141
5Medium

Last hits on a bell curve

Your last hits at 10 minutes follow N(60, 10²). (a) What are the mean and variance? (b) Which is bigger: P(X = 60) or P(55 ≤ X ≤ 65)? (c) Can the density p(x) of some distribution be 4?

Hint

For a continuous X, only areas are probabilities. A single point has width 0.

Solution
  • (a) mean 60, variance 100 (σ = 10)
  • (b) P(X = 60) = 0, while P(55 ≤ X ≤ 65) is the area within half a σ of the center, about 0.38. The interval wins.
  • (c) Yes. Uniform on [0, 0.25] has height 4, because width × height = 1.

Name the distribution

Choose Bernoulli, Binomial, Geometric, Categorical, Beta or Gaussian for each: (i) whether first blood happens before minute 2; (ii) successful neutral-camp stacks out of 10 tries; (iii) which of 5 heroes the enemy bans first; (iv) your belief about your stack success rate; (v) your GPM across 200 games; (vi) games you lose before your first win.

Hint

Ask: yes/no? a count out of n? a label? a probability between 0 and 1? any number near a center? waiting for a success?

Solution
  • (i) Bernoulli · (ii) Binomial · (iii) Categorical
  • (iv) Beta, a distribution over a rate · (v) Gaussian, roughly · (vi) Geometric, failures version
7Challenge · preview of Day 3

A Beta belief gets updated

Your belief about a new Pudge's hook hit rate is Beta(6, 4). (a) Its mean and variance? (b) Then you watch 5 hits and 5 misses, and your belief becomes Beta(11, 9). New mean? Did the curve get wider or narrower?

Hint

Mean a/(a + b), variance ab / ((a + b)²(a + b + 1)). Check (b) in the explorer with the Beta tab.

Solution
  • (a) mean 6/10 = 0.6, variance 24/(100·11) ≈ 0.0218 (sd ≈ 0.148)
  • (b) mean 11/20 = 0.55, variance 99/(400·21) ≈ 0.0118 (sd ≈ 0.109): narrower. More data, more certainty. Adding counts to a and b is exactly tomorrow's topic.
